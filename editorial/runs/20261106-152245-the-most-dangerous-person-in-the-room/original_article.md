Last year, AI made answers free.

Nobody told the org chart.

## 7:52 a.m.

Maya arrives early. Not to impress anyone. She just wants to read the onboarding wiki before the coffee runs out. She finds a page, last updated in 2019, explaining how the monthly report works. She reads it twice. It still doesn't make sense.

She writes one word in the margin of her notebook: Why?

## 9:00 a.m.

Weekly leadership sync. Maya is there to "observe." The monthly report comes up. It takes three departments, four days and one analyst named Dev, who looks like he hasn't slept since the previous fiscal year.

The company also bought an enterprise AI licence in January. Everyone has it. The report still takes four days.

Maya raises her hand. Actually raises it, like in school.

"Who reads this report?"

Silence.

Before the VP can answer, Graham does. Graham has been here fourteen years. Graham has opinions on everything, including things that haven't happened yet. Graham has never, in recorded history, said the words "I don't know."

"With respect," he says, leaning back, "some of us have been doing this a long time."

## 9:14 a.m.

Someone checks anyway. In six months, the report's PDF has been opened by exactly two people. One of them was Dev, checking that it uploaded.

Graham says the data must be wrong.

## 9:31 a.m.

The meeting ends. The immune system wakes up.

A calendar invite lands in Maya's inbox: "Quick sync re: process questions." Her manager is copied. So is HR. Sent by Graham.

## 10:05 a.m.

Graham stops by her desk, smiling. "Love the fresh eyes! But we tried changing the report in 2017. Didn't stick." He leaves before she can ask why.

Every organization has a Graham. Sometimes it's a Grace. I call them Divas, and they come in every gender, every level, every department. They finish your sentences, usually wrong. They explain your idea back to you, slower. If the culture is the immune system, the Diva is the fever: hot, loud and absolutely convinced it's helping.

## 10:30 a.m.

Maya finds Dev at the coffee machine. She doesn't pitch anything. She asks him what the report is supposed to tell people. Dev talks for twenty minutes. Nobody has asked him that in three years. He gets a little emotional near the oat milk.

Then he lowers his voice. "Careful. The last person who asked questions like that lasted eight months. They didn't fire her. They just stopped inviting her to things. Everyone said she wasn't a fit."

## 12:15 p.m.

Lunch. The team heads out together. Nobody asks Maya. Maybe they forgot. Maybe they didn't.

She eats a sandwich at her desk and sketches a one-page version of the report on a napkin: the five numbers that actually matter, what changed, and what someone should do about it.

She also opens LinkedIn. Just to look. Just in case.

## 1:20 p.m.

In the team chat, someone posts a GIF of a detective with a magnifying glass. "Our new auditor is on the case." Six laughing emojis. One of them is Graham's.

Maya adds one too. What else do you do?

## 1:22 p.m.

Here's what's happening, even if nobody would describe it this way.

The organization is reacting to Maya the way a body reacts to something foreign. It doesn't attack her directly. It surrounds her. It isolates her. It sends small, deniable signals until the strange new thing either conforms or leaves.

In a culture that worships perfection, the status quo becomes sacred. The curious employee doesn't look like a cure. She looks like an infection.

There's a medical name for an immune system that attacks something healthy. It's not protection. It's autoimmune disease.

## 2:00 p.m.

She almost doesn't show the napkin to her manager. Her hands are a little shaky. She does it anyway.

Her manager does the most important thing a manager can do in this decade. She doesn't say "that's not how we do things." She says: "Tell me more." And then: "Ignore the sync invite. I'll handle Graham."

One sentence. That's all it takes to stop an immune response.

## 3:45 p.m.

Maya and Dev open the AI tool the company bought in January. Nobody had pointed it at the report, because nobody had asked whether the report should exist in its current form.

They feed it the raw data and the napkin. By 4:20 they have a rough version. It's ugly. It's also four days faster and roughly forty times more readable.

The AI did the work in minutes. The question took a new hire, two months and a napkin.

## 4:30 p.m.

The VP walks by. He looks at the screen. He looks at Maya. He looks at Dev, who is, for the first time in recorded history, smiling.

"Who asked for this?"

"Nobody," says Maya. "I just asked why."

## 5:10 p.m.

The VP opens a blank document. He writes a list of everything his team does "because that's how we've always done it." Then a second list: the names of every curious person who left in the last three years. Next to most of them, without meaning to, he remembers a meeting where Graham spoke last.

Then he pulls up the AI rollout dashboard. Licences: 100% deployed. Measurable impact: a rounding error. He'd blamed the tool.

He looks at the two lists. For the first time, he wonders if the tool was never the problem.

## 7:15 p.m.

At home, his five-year-old daughter is building something out of cereal boxes.

"Why does the moon follow the car?"

"Why do we have eyebrows?"

"Why do you look tired?"

He hears himself start to say "Because." He stops.

He thinks about Graham, who must have been five once too. Who must have asked about the moon. Somewhere along the way, someone rewarded him for being certain instead of curious, and he never looked back.

In The Great Corporate Unmasking, I wrote about the masks we wear at work. The Diva's mask is certainty. Worn long enough, it stops being a mask. It becomes the face. And a face that can't say "I don't know" can't see anything new.

The VP sits down on the floor. "I don't know," he tells his daughter. "Let's find out."

## 11:47 p.m.

Maya can't sleep. She replays the GIF. The lunch. Graham's "with respect." She drafts a message to her manager: "Sorry if I overstepped today."

She doesn't send it.

Tomorrow, she'll ask another question.

## Why this is now an AI problem

The danger in the room used to come from power: who could fire you, promote you, ignore you.

Now it comes from curiosity. From the person who isn't impressed by "the way things are done" and won't stop asking until she understands.

AI changed the economics. It is an answer machine, and answers are now close to free. That quietly bankrupts the Diva, whose entire currency was sounding certain. A model can produce confident answers all day. What it can't do is notice the report nobody opens.

Hand AI to a culture that punishes "why" and you get the same broken process, just faster. That's why so many companies have rolled out the tools and are still waiting for the returns. Gallup's 2026 workplace report makes the same point: the technology works, but the bottom-line gains aren't arriving at scale.

Answers are a commodity now. Questions are the moat.

## The bill

For the person on the receiving end, the immune response is not invisible. Nearly half of American adults have been bullied at work or watched it happen, and those targeted face a 62% chance of losing their job.

Companies pay too. In MIT Sloan's analysis of employee reviews, toxic culture predicted attrition 10.4 times more strongly than pay. Global engagement fell to 20% in 2025, a loss Gallup puts at about $10 trillion. And the people expected to protect the Mayas are running on empty: manager engagement dropped from 27% to 22% in a single year.

The companies that can't turn the ship around rarely lack ideas, or tools. They've let their Divas throw the people with the questions overboard.

So tomorrow morning, look around your meeting.

Find your Maya. Protect her.

Find your Graham. Be patient with him. He was five once too.

Then ask yourself the hardest question of all: when was the last time you said "I don't know"?

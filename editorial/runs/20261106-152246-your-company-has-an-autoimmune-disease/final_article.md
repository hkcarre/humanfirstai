For twenty years, I knew exactly who to fear in a meeting.

It was the person at the head of the table. The one whose silence could end a project. Whose raised eyebrow could rewrite a quarter. Whose "let's take this offline" meant your idea had just been taken out back.

Then AI made answers free, and I realized I'd been watching the wrong chair.

Last spring, the most dangerous person in the room was sitting by the door. Two months into the job, holding a notebook she'd clearly bought for the occasion. And she asked: "Why do we do it this way?"

You know the silence that follows that question. It isn't hostile. It's worse. It's fourteen grown professionals realizing that nobody actually knows.

We'd been running the same approval process for six years. Four sign-offs. Three spreadsheets. One shared folder named "FINAL_v7_REAL_final." Nobody designed it. It just accumulated, like sediment, or emails from your bank.

We'd also just rolled out an enterprise AI tool to the whole team. It was helping us fill in those three spreadsheets faster.

The boss gave the textbook answer: "It's how we ensure quality."

She nodded, smiled and asked again, kindly: "Does it, though?"

That's when I watched the immune system kick in.

## The antibodies

Organizations are a lot like bodies. They're built to survive, and their first instinct when something unfamiliar shows up is to surround it, isolate it and get rid of it.

It never looks like an attack.

It looks like a meeting invite titled "Quick Alignment."

It looks like "Love the energy! Let's park this for Q3."

It looks like her name quietly disappearing from the next project kickoff.

Cultures obsessed with perfection mistake familiarity for health. If it's always been done this way, it must be safe. If it's new, it must be a threat.

## The Diva

Every immune system has its loudest cell. In most organizations, that's the Diva.

Yes, I chose the word on purpose, and no, it has no gender. Divas come in every gender, level and department. They finish your sentences, usually wrong. They "just want to push back a little" on ideas they haven't finished hearing. They have never once said "I don't know" in a meeting, and they're quietly proud of it.

If the culture is the immune system, the Diva is the fever: hot, loud and absolutely convinced it's helping.

Ours was Richard. Eleven years in. When the new hire asked her question, Richard leaned back and said, "With respect, some of us have been doing this a long time." It was the most expensive sentence anyone said that year.

In [The Great Corporate Unmasking](/the-great-corporate-unmasking), I wrote about the masks we wear at work. The Diva's mask is certainty. Worn long enough, it stops being a mask. It becomes the face.

Here's what nobody tells you about the Diva: they were curious once. Every one of us was. Watch any four-year-old. Why is the sky blue? Why do dogs bark? Why can't I have ice cream for dinner? Children don't ask to win. They ask because the world is fascinating and they're not embarrassed to say they don't understand it yet.

Somewhere between the playground and the boardroom, the Diva learned that being right gets you promoted and being curious gets you questioned. So they stopped asking. And a person who has stopped asking can only see what they already know.

## Why AI changes everything

For most of corporate history, the Diva's certainty was worth something. Knowing the answer was scarce. Experience was the moat.

AI just drained it.

Answers are now close to free. A tool will give you a confident, well-formatted answer to almost anything in seconds. Certainty, the Diva's entire currency, has been commoditized.

What AI can't do is notice that two of the four sign-offs have never once rejected anything. It can't ask whether the report should exist. It will run your broken process beautifully, at machine speed, forever.

That's the trap most companies are in right now. They bought the tools and pointed them at processes nobody had questioned. [Gallup's 2026 global workplace report](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx) shows the gap. In US organizations using AI, 65% of workers report a personal productivity gain. Only 12% strongly agree it has changed how work gets done.

The missing ingredient isn't compute. It's the person who asks why. And that is exactly the person your immune system is trained to remove.

## The wound

Being expelled by an organization's immune system doesn't feel like a policy decision. It feels personal, because it is.

The lunch invitations stop. A new Slack channel appears, and she isn't in it. Someone starts calling her "our little auditor," and everyone laughs, and she laughs too, because what else do you do? Her questions get described as "tone." Her ideas get described as "not quite a culture fit."

Nobody shouts. Nobody has to.

She started rehearsing meetings in the shower. She stopped asking questions, then stopped sleeping properly, then started wondering whether the problem was her. That's the cruelest trick of the immune response: it doesn't just remove the curious person. It convinces them they deserved it.

If you've been there, you're far from alone. Bullying is the extreme case of the same reflex. In the [Workplace Bullying Institute's 2024 US survey](https://workplacebullying.org/2024-wbi-us-survey/), 32% of adults said they had been bullied at work. By the same survey's estimate, 62% of targets lose their job, counting those who resign.

The person who gets attacked is usually the one who leaves.

## The bill

Companies tell themselves this is about protecting standards. The numbers tell a different story.

In [MIT Sloan Management Review's 2022 analysis](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/toxic-culture-is-driving-the-great-resignation/) of employee reviews, toxic culture predicted attrition 10.4 times more strongly than compensation. You cannot pay your way out of a culture that bites.

Global engagement fell to 20% in 2025, matching the lowest level since 2020, a drag [Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx) prices at around $10 trillion in lost productivity. Flip it around. In [Gallup's 2024 meta-analysis](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/321725/gallup-q12-meta-analysis.aspx), business units in the top quartile of engagement showed a median 23% higher profitability than those in the bottom quartile.

I can't prove that any single Diva tanked any single share price. But I keep seeing the same pattern. Flat revenue, an AI rollout with nothing to show for it, and a list of curious people who left. Look at who's talking in the meetings, and who's leaving.

There's a medical term for an immune system that attacks something healthy. It's not protection. It's autoimmune disease.

## The turn

She nearly left. She told me later she'd drafted the resignation email twice.

What saved her wasn't persistence. It was one person who decided to act less like an antibody and more like a host. Her manager pulled her aside and said: "Keep asking. I'll take the heat."

Within a month, she had mapped the whole process. She found that two of the four sign-offs had never once rejected anything. Then, with the same AI tool we'd been using to fill in spreadsheets, she built a prototype. In our case, it did in an afternoon what used to take us eleven days.

Same tool. Different question.

## What I've learned since

1. Authority used to come from having answers. AI ended that. Authority now comes from asking the question nobody else will.
2. Your AI strategy is a culture strategy. Automating a process nobody has questioned just makes it fail faster.
3. Your culture has an immune system. Find out what it's attacking. If your most curious people keep leaving, they aren't the infection. They're the symptom.
4. Humility is the door curiosity walks through. You can't learn what you're certain you already know.
5. Exclusion is never "just banter." The nickname, the missing invite, the pause before her name: individually deniable, collectively devastating.
6. Every curious employee needs one protector. Not a committee. One person with enough standing to say "let her finish." That person is usually a manager, and [global manager engagement fell from 27% to 22%](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx) in a single year. Protect the protectors.

So here's my advice, from someone who spent two decades fearing the wrong person: find the most curious person on your team. Then make sure your organization isn't quietly trying to cure itself of them.

And if you're the curious one, reading this at 11 p.m. and wondering if you're the problem:

You're not. You're just the only adult in the room who still remembers how to be a child.

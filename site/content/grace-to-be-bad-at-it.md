Crazy question: when was the last time you were a total beginner at something, in front of strangers?

For me it was September, and I was the one teaching. Yes, at an AI conference. Stick with me.

Claude Community House was four days in Barcelona for people who build with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant. Nearly everything on the schedule happened on a screen. Our session was called Crochet Your Own Claude: two hours, a hook, some yarn, no laptops. We capped it at 15. Twenty-one people showed up anyway.

I ran it with Christina Pruneski, who taught me to crochet and is still much better at it than I am. Everyone made Clawd, a little crab — say it out loud — from a free pattern by Lizette at Chrisette Designs. Go [buy her a coffee](https://ko-fi.com/chrisettedesigns).

Then came the magic ring. It’s how you start anything round, and every beginner pattern warns you about it. Beginners hold the yarn in a death grip, the ring slips, and the whole thing comes apart. Ours came apart a lot.


Nobody could ask an AI to fix a dropped stitch. So people did one of two things. They kept fumbling with their own hands. Or they turned to the stranger beside them and asked for help. Out loud, in a room full of people who build AI for a living.

Being bad at something, and asking for help. Those are the two moves most of us avoid with AI, and both take a little grace.

## So why do we avoid them?

With AI especially, it can feel like everyone else already knows what they’re doing, so being a beginner feels like something to hide. Some people even hide using it at all.

```figure
hidden-half
```

The fear is simple: if I’m bad at this, people will think less of me. The research on both moves is kinder than that.

## What if being bad at it is the point?

One of my slides for the session said this.

> There is no prompt for this. The minutes you spend being bad at it are the skill.

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork have a name for the idea: [desirable difficulty](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf). Practice that feels slow and clumsy in the moment tends to stick longer than practice that feels easy. All that fumbling with the magic ring? Probably learning, the whole time.

You don’t need a study to know you can be bad at something in front of an audience and keep going.

Julia Child [did it on television in 1963](https://saturdayeveningpost.com/2012/08/11/archives/then-and-now/julia-child.html). She tried to flip a potato pancake and it fell apart in the pan. She scooped it back together, said more or less that you need the courage of your convictions when you flip, and carried on.

## And what about asking for help?

The stranger next to you is more likely to say yes than you think. Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake found that people [underestimate by as much as half](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128) how likely strangers are to agree when asked.

One attendee said it better than I could.

```testimony
It was an amazing parallel to the adaptation moment we’ve been through in the AI era. It’s interesting to realise how much you can accomplish when you focus, and also ask for help.
— An attendee, Claude Community House
```

## So where do we go from here?

This is where the grace comes in, and it starts with you. In [one study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599), students failed a hard test. Some were told they weren’t alone, and shouldn’t be too hard on themselves. They studied about 50% longer before their second try than the students who got no message at all.

```figure
grace-arc
```

Then give the same grace to the next beginner. When the person next to you is stuck on their first question to a chatbot, or their first magic ring, sit with them. Keep your hands in your lap. Let them be bad at it for a few minutes, and let them get it right on their own.

**Try this.** Pick one AI tool you have been avoiding. Give yourself twenty minutes to be bad at it, and ask someone to sit with you while you do.

Then come back and tell us which tool you picked, and how bad you were at it. No judgment here.
